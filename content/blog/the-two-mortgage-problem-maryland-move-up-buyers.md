Dana and Mark Whitfield have lived in the same house off Old Westminster Pike for eleven years, and they're ready for the next one - a little more land, a real office, a bedroom their teenager doesn't have to negotiate for. They have the equity. They have the motivation. What stops them cold is the same thing that stalls half the move-up buyers I talk to: the mortgage they refinanced at 3.1% in 2021, and the very reasonable fear of being on the hook for two of them at once.

That 3.1% is the whole story. It's cheap money they'll never see again, and walking away from it to carry a second loan at today's rates - even for a few weeks - is the math that keeps good buyers parked in houses they've outgrown.

So let's actually work the problem, because there isn't one answer. There are three, and the right one depends on your timeline and your tolerance for uncertainty far more than your bank balance.

> You're selling into a market where you have leverage and buying into one where you don't have much time. The whole game is closing the gap between the two houses without paying for it twice.

**Quick answer:** Maryland move-up buyers bridge the gap between houses three ways. **Sell first** and rent your home back from the buyer for up to 59 days, so your equity is real cash before you shop - the cleanest, cheapest route. **Buy first** using a bridge loan, a HELOC, an 80-10-10 loan, or a "buy before you sell" program to tap your equity early - faster, but you pay somewhere between 2% and 11% for it. Or write a **contingent offer** - the weakest option in a tight market, and usually saddled with a 48-to-72-hour kick-out clause. Which one fits comes down to whether you've already found the house you can't lose.

## Why the two-mortgage problem is worse right now

Rates haven't done move-up buyers any favors this year. The 30-year fixed has been sitting in the high 6s - 6.71% on the most recent Freddie Mac survey - and forecasters don't expect much movement before the new year. That's not catastrophic. It's just enough to turn the math on carrying two houses at once from *annoying* into *genuinely uncomfortable*.

At the same time, Carroll, Howard, and Frederick Counties are still tight. Not 2021 tight, where you'd lose a house to eleven other offers by Sunday brunch - but tight enough that a well-priced home in a strong school district still trades in a few weeks, not a few months. Statewide, Maryland is sitting around two and a half to three months of supply, and anything under five or six months is still a seller's market.

Put those two facts together and you get the real squeeze. You're selling into a market where you have leverage, and buying into one where you don't have much time to sit around waiting for your equity to land in your account first. Every one of the options below is a different answer to that timing problem.

## Option 1: Sell first, and use a rent-back to buy yourself room

This is the one I steer most people toward, and it's simpler than it sounds. You list your current home, you sell it, and as part of the deal you negotiate to stay in it as a paying tenant of your own buyer - anywhere from a couple of weeks up to about 59 days.

Why 59 and not 60? Because most lenders' owner-occupancy rules kick in right at the 60-day mark, and nobody wants to discover that three days before they're supposed to hand over the keys. Agents who've been burned by it cap rent-backs at 59 on purpose.

The advantage is that you're not guessing. Your equity is real money in an account, not a projection. You know exactly what you're working with when you go make an offer on the next place, and depending on the market you may be able to write that offer without a financing contingency tied to a sale that hasn't happened yet - which makes it read a lot more like a cash offer to the seller on the other side. The rent-back itself gets negotiated as part of your sale contract; [what happens after your home goes under contract](/blog/what-happens-after-your-home-goes-under-contract) walks through where it fits in the timeline.

The tradeoff is obvious: you need somewhere to land if your next house isn't ready the day the rent-back ends. Sometimes that's a short-term rental. Sometimes it's family for three weeks and a storage unit. It isn't glamorous, but it's the cheapest, cleanest version of this whole process, and it's why it's usually my first suggestion for anyone who isn't in a rush measured in days.

## Option 2: Buy first, using your equity before the sale closes

If a 30-day gap between houses sounds like a personal nightmare, there are a few ways to get at your current equity before your old house actually sells. All of them cost something; the question is how much, and how much certainty you're buying with it.

## Option 3: The contingent offer, and why it's a harder sell now

The traditional move - making your purchase contingent on selling your current home - still exists, but it's the weakest offer you can put in front of a Maryland seller in this market.

Most sellers who will accept one insist on a **kick-out clause**: it gives them the right to keep marketing their house, and it gives you 48 to 72 hours to drop your contingency and proceed unconditionally if a stronger offer walks in the door. If you can't produce the cash on that timeline, you lose the house.

There's a lesser-known Maryland form worth knowing about here too - the **Seller's Home of Choice Addendum**. It works the other direction: it protects a seller who is also trying to buy their next home, by making the sale of their current property contingent on their own purchase actually closing. If you're selling and buying at the same time, this is the piece that keeps you from ending up sold, cashed out, and with nowhere to go.

## Which one is actually right for the Whitfields?

Honestly? Probably the rent-back. They have equity, they aren't in a rush measured in days, and a 30-to-45-day cushion after closing gives them room to find the right next house without a bridge loan's interest rate quietly eating the equity that move was supposed to buy them in the first place.

But that's their situation, not a universal answer. If you're the buyer who has already found the house - the one you would genuinely lose sleep over losing to someone else's offer - the math on a bridge loan or a HELOC starts to look a lot more reasonable. Certainty on a specific home is worth paying for; certainty in the abstract usually isn't. If you're still working out whether the next house is even in range, [how much cash you actually need to buy in Maryland](/blog/how-much-money-do-you-need-to-buy-a-house-in-maryland) and [what $500K actually buys in Carroll County right now](/blog/what-buying-above-500k-actually-looks-like-in-carroll-county-maryland) are the better places to start.

## What I'd line up before you list

- Get a real number on your current home's value **and** your net proceeds after loan payoff and selling costs - not a Zestimate, an actual comparative market analysis from recent closed sales in your neighborhood
- If a HELOC is even a maybe, open it now, before you list - the window closes once the house is on the market
- Ask your lender to price a bridge loan and a "buy before you sell" program side by side, so you know the real cost of buying first rather than guessing at it
- Decide your housing-gap plan in advance: where you'd stay for two to six weeks if the timing doesn't line up cleanly
- Talk to an agent who has actually run both sides of a simultaneous sale-and-purchase in Carroll, Howard, or Frederick County - the coordination is where these deals succeed or fall apart

## The Bottom Line

The two-mortgage fear is real, but it's solvable, and you don't have to pick blind. Selling first with a rent-back is the cheapest fix and fits most move-up buyers. Buying first costs somewhere between 2% and 11%, and what you're buying with it is certainty on one specific house. A contingent offer is a last resort in a market this tight. Choose based on your timeline and how much uncertainty you can actually stomach - and set it up before the sign goes in the yard, not after.

## Frequently Asked Questions: Buying Before You Sell in Maryland

**Can I buy a house before I sell my current one in Maryland?**

Yes. A bridge loan, a HELOC, an 80-10-10 loan structure, or a "buy before you sell" program each let you tap your current equity for a down payment before the sale closes. Every option carries a cost - roughly 2% to 11% depending on the tool and how long you carry it - and a HELOC generally has to be opened before you list your home. For the full picture of the cash a purchase requires beyond the down payment, see [how much money you actually need to buy a house in Maryland](/blog/how-much-money-do-you-need-to-buy-a-house-in-maryland).

**What is a rent-back, and how long can it last?**

A rent-back (or post-settlement occupancy) lets you sell your home and then stay in it as a paying tenant of the buyer for a set period - commonly a couple of weeks up to about 59 days. Experienced agents cap it at 59 rather than 60 because many lenders' owner-occupancy requirements for the buyer begin at the 60-day mark.

**How much does a bridge loan cost in 2026?**

Bridge loans are currently running roughly 8.5% to 11% for a 6-to-12-month term, plus an origination fee. On a $500,000 loan that's a meaningful number, which is why buyers weigh it against a HELOC (around 7% to 9.5%) or against selling first and using a rent-back.

**What is a kick-out clause?**

It's a provision a seller adds when accepting a contingent offer. The seller keeps their home on the market, and if a stronger offer comes in, you have 48 to 72 hours to remove your home-sale contingency and proceed unconditionally. If you can't, the seller can accept the other offer and you lose the house. It's one of several pieces of contract language that decide competitive Maryland offers - [escalation clauses and appraisal gaps](/blog/the-clause-that-wins-bidding-wars-escalation-and-appraisal-gaps-maryland) are the others worth understanding before you write one.

**What is the Seller's Home of Choice Addendum in Maryland?**

It's a Maryland contract addendum that protects a seller who is also buying their next home. It makes the sale of their current property contingent on their own replacement purchase actually closing, so they aren't left having sold with nowhere to move. If you're selling and buying at the same time, it's the clause that keeps the two transactions tied together.
