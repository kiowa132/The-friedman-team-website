Picture a couple named Jordan and Alex. They rent a three-bedroom house in Baltimore County, they both have steady jobs, and they have talked about buying for two years. What has stopped them is a list of things they have heard: you need 20 percent down, you need perfect credit, rates are too high, renting is cheaper anyway. Some of it is half true. Most of it is out of date.

Here are the five myths I hear most from first-time buyers, and what is actually true.

**Quick answer:** You do not need 20 percent down, you do not need great credit, renting is not always cheaper, pre-qualified and pre-approved are not the same thing, and waiting for perfect rates is a bet, not a plan. The fastest way to find out where you stand is to get pre-approved and run the real monthly payment.

## Myth 1: Do You Need 20 Percent Down to Buy a House?

No. Twenty percent down is not a requirement for most buyers. It does let you skip mortgage insurance, but that is a choice, not a rule.

That second number includes principal and interest, property taxes, insurance, and mortgage insurance on a $300,000 home with 5 percent down, at about 6.95 percent. In early July, when rates averaged around 6.4 percent, the same purchase came to about $2,250 a month.

Two things make the comparison lopsided. First, a fixed-rate mortgage locks your principal and interest for 30 years, while rent usually goes up every year. Second, your rent buys you nothing at the end, while a mortgage payment builds equity. Buying is not right for everyone, and it depends on how long you will stay, but the idea that renting is automatically cheaper is not true here.

## Myth 3: Do You Need Great Credit to Qualify?

You need workable credit, not perfect credit. The general minimums by loan type:

- **Conventional:** generally 620 or higher.
- **FHA:** 580 or higher with 3.5 percent down.
- **VA:** no official minimum, so it depends on the lender.
- **USDA:** typically 640 or higher.

Keep in mind that lenders can set their own stricter minimums, so the score that works in practice can sit a little above the official floor. The right move is to talk to a lender early. Many buyers find they are closer than they thought, and a lender can tell you exactly what to improve and how long it will take.

## Myth 5: Should You Wait Because Rates Are Too High?

Waiting for the perfect rate is a bet on something nobody can predict. In early July, the 30-year fixed rate averaged about 6.4 percent. Since then it has climbed to just under 7 percent. Buyers who waited for a better rate got a worse one.

The better approach is to calculate your actual payment at today's rate and see if it fits your budget. If it does, buying now makes sense. If rates fall later, you may be able to refinance. If prices rise while you wait, you cannot go back.

## The Bottom Line

Every one of these myths has the same effect: it convinces a qualified buyer to wait. Waiting has a price too, in rent paid, equity not built, and prices that keep moving. The way out is not a leap of faith. It is a short conversation with a lender and a spreadsheet with real numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much do I need to put down on my first home in Maryland?**

Often as little as 3 to 3.5 percent with a conventional or FHA loan, and nothing with VA or USDA loans if you qualify. The median first-time buyer nationally put down about 10 percent in 2025. Maryland's Mortgage Program also offers down payment assistance to eligible first-time buyers.

**What credit score do I need to buy a house?**

Around 620 for most conventional loans, 580 for an FHA loan with 3.5 percent down, and roughly 640 for USDA. Individual lenders can require more, so ask a lender early.

**Is it cheaper to rent or buy in Baltimore County?**

It depends on the home and your down payment, but the gap is smaller than many people expect. A $300,000 home with 5 percent down costs about the same each month as renting a three-bedroom house right now, and buying builds equity.

**What is the difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval?**

Pre-qualification is an estimate from information you provide. Pre-approval is verified by a lender who reviews your income, assets, and credit. Sellers give pre-approval much more weight.

**Should I wait for mortgage rates to drop before buying?**

Rates are hard to predict, and waiting has its own costs. Work out the monthly payment at today's rate. If it fits your budget, buying now is reasonable, and you can look at refinancing if rates fall.
