Picture buyers named Mark and Priya. They offer $612,000 on a five-acre property in Sykesville: a renovated farmhouse and a barn the sellers converted into a beautiful three-stall setup, with a tack room and rubber matting. The offer is accepted. Then the appraisal comes back at $585,000.

That is a $27,000 gap, and the reason behind it is what makes this story worth reading. The appraiser dismissed the barn as having "minimal contributory value" and pulled comparable sales from subdivision homes six miles away, none with acreage and none with a barn. The bank was not valuing this property. It was valuing a different one.

**Quick answer:** If a home appraises below your contract price, you have real options: ask the lender for a Reconsideration of Value with better comparable sales, renegotiate the price, cover part of the gap yourself, or walk away if you kept your appraisal contingency. On unusual properties like farms and horse properties, a well-documented reconsideration request is often the strongest first move.

## What Happens When a Home Appraises Below the Contract Price?

The lender will only lend against the appraised value, not the price you agreed to. If the appraisal is lower, the loan amount shrinks, and someone has to close the difference. That means the deal now needs a decision, and there are four ways it can go:

Most buyers jump straight to the second and third options, which means they pay to fix a problem that may not be real. In Mark and Priya's case, the appraisal was not measuring the property they were buying.

## Why Are Appraisal Gaps Happening in 2026?

Fewer homes are selling, which means fewer recent comparable sales for an appraiser to use. When the nearby data is thin, appraisers stretch to older sales or to homes that are only loosely similar, and the appraisal software they rely on handles unusual properties poorly.

Low appraisals are not the norm. Industry estimates put them at roughly one in ten purchase appraisals. But they cluster in exactly the places where comparable sales are hardest to find: acreage, custom builds, waterfront, and homes with unusual features. If your property is one of a kind, your risk is higher than average.

## What Is a Reconsideration of Value, and Does It Work?

A Reconsideration of Value, or ROV, is a formal request asking the lender to have the appraisal looked at again, backed by evidence the appraiser did not use. It is not an appeal in the courtroom sense and it is not a complaint. It is a documented case that better comparable sales exist.

Under rules published by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and HUD in 2024, the process is standardized:

- A borrower can request one ROV per appraisal.
- The request can include up to five alternative comparable sales the borrower believes better represent the property.
- The lender has to have a review process and assign an appraisal expert to look at the request.

That structure is why ROVs work when they are done well. A vague "the number seems low" goes nowhere. A request that lists specific sales the appraiser missed, with the reasons they are comparable, gives the reviewer something concrete to weigh.

For the Sykesville farm, we submitted an ROV with three comparable sales the appraiser had missed. Two of them had similar acreage and equestrian improvements and had sold in the previous ten months. One was 15 miles away, which is a stretch on a suburban home but reasonable when nothing closer looks like a horse property. The revised appraisal came back at $604,000.

The remaining $8,000 gap was split between the buyers and sellers through a price reduction. Mark and Priya went from a $27,000 problem to an $8,000 one that both sides could split.

## Why Do Barns, Arenas, and Upgrades Get Undervalued?

Appraisers value improvements by their contributory value, meaning what the improvement adds to what buyers will actually pay, not what it cost to build. A custom barn can cost far more than the market will credit it for, especially when nearby sales do not include anything like it.

That is the over-improvement trap. If you put $80,000 into a heated indoor arena, a finished basement with a home theater, or a saltwater pool on a property where nothing comparable exists nearby, you should not expect to get every one of those dollars back at appraisal time. The market decides what an improvement is worth, and in a neighborhood where nobody else has one, the market has no reference point.

Unusual properties also depend heavily on who is doing the appraising. Fannie Mae requires that an appraiser have geographic competency, meaning real knowledge of the local market and property type, before accepting an assignment. There is no fixed mileage that guarantees it, so an appraiser who spends most of the year in subdivisions can miss what a horse property is worth to the buyers who want one. For income-producing equestrian properties like boarding barns, appraisers may also consider an income approach, which looks at the rent the facility can generate.

## How Can Buyers and Sellers Protect Themselves From a Low Appraisal?

Some of this is decided before an appraiser ever visits. A few habits make a real difference on unusual properties.

1. **Keep your appraisal contingency on acreage and barn properties.** These are the properties where a low appraisal is most likely, and the contingency is what lets you renegotiate or walk away instead of finding an extra $27,000 on short notice.
2. **Ask for an appraiser who knows the property type.** Your lender orders the appraisal, but your agent can flag early that the property is not a standard subdivision home so the right professional is assigned.
3. **Have your comparable sales ready before the appraisal.** If your agent already knows which sales best support the price, an ROV is a matter of days, not weeks.
4. **Provide a list of improvements.** A short sheet showing the barn, arena, or upgrades, with dates and costs, gives the appraiser context they may not otherwise have.
5. **Price by what the market supports, not by what improvements cost.** This is advice for sellers. Overpricing based on your build costs is the surest way to set up a gap.

## The Bottom Line

The appraisal is an opinion, and on unusual properties it is an opinion formed from thin data. That is not a reason to panic and it is not a reason to accept the first number. Mark and Priya's $27,000 gap was not a verdict on their farm. It was a research gap, and the right comparable sales closed most of it. If your appraisal comes back low, the first question should be whether the appraiser saw the whole picture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What happens if my home appraises for less than the contract price?**

The lender will only lend based on the appraised value, so the buyer and seller need to resolve the difference. Options include challenging the appraisal with a Reconsideration of Value, renegotiating the price, the buyer covering part of the gap, or terminating the contract if there is an appraisal contingency.

**How does a Reconsideration of Value work?**

The borrower submits a written request through the lender with supporting comparable sales the appraiser did not use, up to five. The lender reviews it with an appraisal expert. A borrower can request one ROV per appraisal, so the first request should be your best one.

**Can I challenge a low appraisal in Maryland?**

Yes. ROV rules are set by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and HUD, so the same process applies across Maryland. Your agent typically gathers the comparable sales and your lender submits the request.

**Will a barn or horse facility add value at appraisal?**

Sometimes, but usually less than it cost to build. Appraisers credit an improvement for what it adds to market value, and on properties without similar nearby sales, that credit can be small. Recent sales of comparable horse properties are the strongest evidence for a higher number.

**Should I keep an appraisal contingency?**

On acreage, barn, or one-of-a-kind properties, yes. The contingency protects your earnest money and gives you room to renegotiate if the appraisal comes back low. Waiving it can make an offer more competitive, but it puts the gap on you.
