Picture a couple named Tom and Renee. They have outgrown their Carroll County home and they keep looking at bigger houses online, doing quick math in their heads, and closing the tab. They assume the new house means the full asking price, a brand new mortgage at today's rate, and a down payment they have not saved. So they stay put.

What they have not done is check what their current home is actually worth. Most people who decide they cannot afford to move up made the decision before running a single real number.

**Quick answer:** You can afford to move up if your usable equity, meaning what you would walk away with after paying off your mortgage and the costs of selling, covers a solid down payment on the next home and the new monthly payment fits your budget. Your equity is often higher than you think, especially if you have not looked at recent sales near you. The whole calculation takes three steps.

## How Do You Calculate the Equity You Can Use to Move Up?

Equity is not what you paid for your home or what the county assessment says. It is what the home would sell for today, minus what you owe, minus what it costs to sell. That last number is the piece most homeowners forget.

What is left is your usable equity, and it becomes your down payment on the next home.

Selling costs vary by county and by how a deal is structured. Maryland seller costs commonly land somewhere between about 6 and 9 percent of the sale price when you count everything. In Carroll County, the state transfer tax is 0.5 percent with no county add-on, while Howard County adds a county transfer tax on top, and the standard Maryland contract splits transfer and recordation taxes evenly between buyer and seller. Our [net proceeds calculator](/calculators/net-proceeds) shows what your specific home would leave you after all of it.

Now they look at a $750,000 home. Closing costs on a conventional purchase are typically 2 to 3 percent of the price, so call it about $18,750. That leaves roughly $197,000 for a down payment, which is about 26 percent down. Their new loan would be around $553,000.

At a 30-year fixed rate of 6.95 percent, close to where Freddie Mac's national average has been sitting recently, principal and interest on that loan is about $3,660 a month. That is a real number and it may be higher than their current payment, but it is a long way from the impossible figure they had in their heads, because the equity did most of the work.

That is the point of the calculation: a strong equity position from your current home offsets a big chunk of the difference between your old rate and today's rate.

## What Costs Do Move-Up Buyers Forget?

The mortgage payment is only part of what changes when you buy a bigger home. Three items catch people off guard:

- **Property taxes.** Your bill is based on the new home's assessed value and the rate where it sits, and those rates differ by jurisdiction. County property tax runs at roughly one percent of assessed value in both Carroll and Howard County, before any municipal tax. On a $750,000 home, one percent is about $625 a month, so check the actual tax bill for any home you are considering.
- **Homeowners insurance.** It scales with square footage and the quality of finishes. A larger, newer home usually costs more to insure than the one you are leaving.
- **Closing costs.** Plan on 2 to 3 percent of the purchase price on a conventional loan, on top of what you spend selling.

Add those to the principal and interest number and you have the true monthly cost. Compare that to what you pay now, all in.

## What If You Would Be Carrying Two Mortgages?

Timing is the second half of the affordability question. If your equity is locked in a home you have not sold yet, you either need to sell first, buy with a contingency, or bridge the gap. ## Why Do So Many Homeowners Think They Cannot Move Up?

Three assumptions get in the way, and they all tilt the math against you:

- They assume they will pay full asking price when many homes sell for less, or that they will be bidding against a crowd.
- They underestimate their equity because they have not checked recent nearby sales. Values change, and it is common to be surprised in the upward direction.
- They compare their current payment to a worst-case new payment instead of running the actual numbers.

## What to Do This Week

1. Get a real market value for your home from recent nearby sales, not an app estimate.
2. Ask your lender for your exact payoff amount.
3. Run your numbers through the [net proceeds calculator](/calculators/net-proceeds) to see your usable equity.
4. Pick a price range for the next home and estimate the full monthly cost, including taxes and insurance.
5. Talk to a lender about what you qualify for, so you know your ceiling before you start touring.

## The Bottom Line

Moving up is rarely as impossible as it feels from the couch. Once you replace guesses with your home's real value, your real payoff, and a realistic estimate of selling costs, most people find they have more buying power than they thought. The only way to know is to run the numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much equity do I need to move up to a bigger home?**

There is no single number. What matters is that your usable equity, after your payoff and selling costs, covers a down payment and closing costs that keep the new payment comfortable. Many buyers aim for at least 10 to 20 percent down, though the right figure depends on your loan and budget.

**How much does it cost to sell a house in Maryland?**

Commonly about 6 to 9 percent of the sale price when you add agent commissions, transfer and recordation taxes, and other seller closing costs. Our net proceeds calculator estimates it for your specific home.

**Can I use my equity as a down payment before I sell?**

Only if you tap it another way, such as a home equity loan or line of credit or a bridge loan. Otherwise the equity is not available until your current home sells and closes.

**Will my monthly payment go up if I buy a bigger home?**

Usually, yes, because of the higher price and today's rates, and property taxes and insurance rise too. A large down payment from your equity can offset a significant part of that increase.

**What should I do first if I want to move up?**

Find out what your home is worth today and what you would net after selling. Everything else in the plan depends on that number.
