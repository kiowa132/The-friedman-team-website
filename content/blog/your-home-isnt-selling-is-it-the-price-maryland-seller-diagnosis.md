Picture a seller named Lisa. Her Parkville home has been listed for six weeks. She has had a handful of showings, no offers, and a growing suspicion that the house is cursed. Her neighbors have opinions. Her brother says drop the price by $20,000 today.

Here is what I tell every seller in that spot: a home that is not selling is not automatically a pricing problem. It can be condition, presentation, marketing, location, buyer demand, or the competition sitting down the street. Cutting the price is a big decision, and it should come from evidence, not frustration.

**Quick answer:** Look at the first two weeks. If showings and saves were very low from the start, or buyers come through and keep choosing a competing home, the price is probably the problem. If showings are steady and buyers are coming back for second visits, patience may be the better move. When you do reduce, make one meaningful cut, not a series of small ones.

## Why Do the First Two Weeks Tell You Almost Everything?

A new listing gets its biggest burst of attention right away. It triggers saved-search alerts, shows up as new on Zillow, Redfin, and Bright MLS, and gets a look from agents who have buyers waiting for exactly this kind of home. That attention does not last, because a home is only new once.

If your home draws little interest in that window, buyers are looking at it and deciding there is better value sitting right next to it. The market has already voted, and a slow month will not change the ballot.

## Are You Comparing to What Sold, or to What Is Live?

This is the most common mistake I see. Sellers anchor to the price the house down the road sold for last spring. Buyers do not. They compare your home to every active listing they can see today, all at once, and weigh square footage, lot size, age, updates, schools, garage, finished basement, HOA, and taxes.

If the home down the road offers more for the same money, that is where the buyer goes. A sold price tells you where the market was. The active listings tell you what you are competing against now.

## What Does Buyer Feedback Really Tell You?

Ignore the one-off comment. One buyer who hated the wallpaper does not matter. A pattern does. When the same things come up in showing after showing, that is the market talking:

- The price feels high for the condition.
- The kitchen or bathrooms are dated.
- The carpet needs replacing.
- There is an odor, too much furniture, dark rooms, or a neglected yard.
- Repairs have been put off.

Buyers also form opinions before they arrive, from your photos, your description, and any video. A weak online presentation costs you showings you never even see.

## How Do Condition and Price Work Together?

Almost any home can sell if the price reflects the condition. A house that needs a new roof can go quickly when it is priced for a new roof. A dated kitchen becomes acceptable when buyers feel they are getting a fair deal. The trouble starts when the price says "move-in ready" and the house does not deliver, because every cosmetic flaw becomes a negotiating point.

## If You Reduce the Price, How Much Should You Cut?

Make it count. Small, incremental cuts of $3,000 or $5,000 rarely help. They keep the home in the same buyer search range, and they tell the market you are willing to keep negotiating. A single meaningful reduction can move your home into entirely new search brackets and put it in front of buyers who had never seen it.

Then comes the trap I call chasing the market. A home lists too high, weeks pass without offers, and the seller drops the price a little at a time while conditions shift. By the time the price is finally right, the early buyers are gone and demand has cooled, and the home sells for less than it would have if it had been priced correctly on day one.

Our own weekly data shows what that looks like. In the week of September 14 to 20, homes that closed in Maryland sold for a median 98.4 percent of their original list price, but 100.0 percent of their final list price. The gap between those two numbers is price cuts made along the way.

Price cuts are also common, so you would not be alone. Redfin reported that 35.4 percent of U.S. sellers cut their asking price in April 2026. But common is not the same as necessary, and the question is always whether your listing has earned one.

## Are You Pricing With Your Heart or With the Market?

I understand the attachment. You raised your family there and you put money into that kitchen. But buyers are not paying for your memories. They are weighing the market value today, the homes they can choose from, what the monthly payment will be, and what condition the house is in. The market sets the price. Not the seller, and honestly, not me either.

## What Questions Should You Ask Before Changing the Price?

Before I ever recommend an adjustment, I walk every seller through the same eight questions:

1. How many showings has the home had?
2. How many offers have come in?
3. What feedback keeps repeating?
4. How does the home compare to the active competition?
5. Have similar nearby homes sold recently, and for how much?
6. Are buyers negotiating hard?
7. Is local inventory rising or falling?
8. How quickly are comparable homes selling?

Rates, seasonality, and the number of active buyers matter too, and they change. A strategy that worked eighteen months ago may not work in today's market. ## The Bottom Line

A price reduction should come from evidence, not frustration. Sometimes the right answer is to wait, sometimes it is to cut once and cut clearly, and sometimes the real fix is presentation or condition. The only way to know which is to look honestly at your showings, your competition, and the feedback you are getting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why isn't my house selling in Maryland?**

The most common reasons are price, condition, weak photos or marketing, and strong competition from nearby listings. Look at showing activity in the first two weeks and the pattern in buyer feedback to figure out which one applies.

**How long should I wait before lowering my price?**

The first two weeks show you most of what you need to know. If interest is very low from the start, waiting longer rarely fixes it. If showings are steady and buyers return for second looks, patience can be reasonable.

**How much should I reduce my price?**

Enough to change which buyers see it. Small cuts of a few thousand dollars usually leave you in the same search range and signal weakness. One meaningful reduction is more effective than several tiny ones.

**Should I price based on what similar homes sold for?**

Sold prices are useful, but buyers compare your home to what is active and available right now. Price against the live competition as well as recent sales.

**Can I sell a house that needs work?**

Yes. Almost any home can sell if the price reflects its condition. Trouble comes when the price suggests a move-in ready home and the condition does not match.
