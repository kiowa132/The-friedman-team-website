> "It can process enormous amounts of data. It cannot see a house."

## The Zestimate Accuracy Problem, in Zillow's Own Words

Zillow publishes its own accuracy data, and it splits into two very different stories depending on one detail: whether the home is currently listed for sale.

For homes actively on the market, the published median error runs close to **2%**. That sounds reassuring until you understand why the number looks that good - once a home is listed, the algorithm has the agent's list price to lean on. It isn't really predicting anything at that point. It's echoing pricing work a human already did.

The number that actually matters - the one for a home that isn't listed, the one nearly everyone is looking at when they check their own address out of curiosity - runs closer to **7% nationally**.

**Quick Math:** On a home worth $600,000 - a market value many Carroll and Howard County homeowners are sitting on right now - a 7% error is a range of roughly **$40,000 to $50,000**. And that's the median. Half of all off-market Zestimates miss by more than that.

It gets worse the further a property sits from a cookie-cutter subdivision with a deep pool of recent comparable sales. Rural markets, older housing stock, custom builds, and properties with acreage regularly see error rates climb into double digits. State-level data shows this clearly: markets with dense, uniform housing see errors as low as 5%, while markets with more unique, spread-out inventory see errors well above 10%.

If that description sounds like a farm outside Taneytown or a historic property in Westminster, you're exactly the kind of homeowner this affects most.

## What an Algorithm Can't See From a Server Farm

This is the part that should be obvious but somehow isn't. Zillow's model pulls from public records, county tax assessments, and past sales data. It has never stood in your kitchen. It doesn't know if you gutted the bathrooms last spring, finished the basement, replaced the roof, or if the HVAC system is twelve years old and on borrowed time.

Kitchen and bathroom remodels - the two projects homeowners point to most often when they talk about the value they've added - frequently happen without a permit pulled. No permit means no public record. No public record means the algorithm has no idea the work ever happened. A $40,000 kitchen renovation might as well not exist as far as a Zestimate is concerned.

### The Luxury Market Blind Spot

The same blind spot shows up at the high end of the market. Automated valuation models consistently underperform on luxury and unique properties, because the things that actually drive value at that level - a premium lot, custom finishes, a specific architectural style, a setting rather than just a square footage number - are exactly the details an algorithm cannot weigh.

If you own a higher-end home in our market, that gap isn't a minor inconvenience. It's potentially tens of thousands of dollars left on the table, or a listing priced in a way that undersells what the property actually offers.

## The Cautionary Tale: When Zillow Bet Real Money on Its Own Algorithm

If you want the cleanest proof that an algorithm isn't a substitute for someone actually seeing a property, you don't have to take an agent's word for it. Take Zillow's.

A few years ago, Zillow decided to put the Zestimate to the ultimate test. Through a division called Zillow Offers, the company used its own valuation model to make instant cash offers on homes and buy them directly - betting that over a decade of data and refinement had made the algorithm reliable enough to price real transactions.

It hadn't. Zillow ended up systematically overpaying for homes it couldn't accurately price in a shifting market.

**What actually happened:**

- In Phoenix alone, the large majority of homes the company had purchased were sitting on the books at a loss
- By the time Zillow shut the division down entirely, the company had written off several hundred million dollars
- A significant share of the workforce was laid off as a result
- Zillow's own CEO acknowledged the algorithm had unintentionally purchased homes at prices higher than they were actually worth

Consider what that means: the company that literally built the Zestimate couldn't trust it enough to buy houses without losing money on a large share of them. That's not a criticism of Zillow's engineers. It's an honest, well-documented example of what any automated valuation model can and cannot do.

## Why This Matters More in Carroll, Baltimore, Howard & Frederick Counties

Automated valuation models perform best in markets with dense, recent, highly comparable sales data - think large subdivisions built within the last fifteen years, where every home shares a handful of floor plans. That isn't the market most of our clients are selling in.

- **Carroll County** includes working farms, equestrian properties, and homes on multiple acres, where the nearest comparable sale might be a mile away and built in a completely different decade
- **Baltimore and Frederick Counties** carry older neighborhoods with character, custom additions, and renovation history that never made it into a public record
- **Howard County** mixes newer high-density development with pockets of older, larger-lot homes just a few streets apart - the exact kind of hyperlocal variation an algorithm averaging over a wide radius will miss

None of this means the Zestimate is useless. It's a reasonable starting point - a rough sense of direction before you've talked to anyone. It shouldn't be the number you use to set expectations, price a listing, or decide whether now is the right time to sell.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How accurate is Zillow's Zestimate?**

Zillow's own published data shows a median error rate of roughly 2% for homes currently listed for sale and roughly 7% for homes that are not listed. Because most homeowners check their Zestimate before ever listing, the more relevant number for planning purposes is the higher, off-market figure - and it can run higher still in rural markets or areas with unique properties.

**Why is my Zestimate different from what my home is actually worth?**

Zestimates are built from public records, tax assessments, and past sales data. They don't account for interior condition, unpermitted renovations, deferred maintenance, or the specific character of a neighborhood. Two homes with identical square footage can have very different values, and an algorithm working from a database has no way to see that difference.

**What's the difference between a Zestimate and a CMA?**

A Zestimate is an automated estimate generated by an algorithm with no direct knowledge of the property. A Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) is built by a licensed agent who has walked the property, verified its condition and updates, and selected truly comparable recent sales rather than a wide statistical average. A CMA accounts for the details that actually move a sale price.

**Should I trust the Zestimate when pricing my home to sell?**

Use it as a rough starting point only. Pricing a home based solely on an automated estimate risks leaving money on the table if the algorithm underestimates the property, or an overpriced listing that sits on the market if it overestimates. An in-person evaluation from a local agent will always be more accurate than an automated model, especially in markets like Carroll, Baltimore, Howard, and Frederick Counties where property types vary widely.
