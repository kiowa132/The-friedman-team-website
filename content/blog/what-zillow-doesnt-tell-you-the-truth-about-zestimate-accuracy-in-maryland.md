A few weeks ago I had a seller pull up her Zestimate on her phone before I'd even walked through the front door. She had been watching that number for months, the way people check a stock ticker. It had moved eighteen thousand dollars in six weeks without her lifting a finger. No renovation, no market shift she could point to. Just an algorithm quietly recalculating in the background and handing her a new answer.



She was not wrong to look. It is free, it is instant, and it shows up the moment you type in an address. But here is the part almost nobody explains: the number she had been anchoring her expectations to was, by Zillow's own admission, the least reliable version of that number Zillow produces.



If you are thinking about selling a home in Carroll, Baltimore, Howard, or Frederick County, understanding why that number can be off by tens of thousands of dollars matters more than almost anything else in the process.



The Zestimate Accuracy Problem, In Zillow's Own Words



Zillow publishes its own accuracy data, and it splits into two very different stories depending on one detail: whether the home is currently listed for sale.



For homes actively on the market, the published median error runs close to 2 percent. That sounds reassuring until you understand why the number looks that good. Once a home is listed, the algorithm has the agent's list price to lean on. It is not really predicting anything at that point. It is echoing pricing work a human already did.



The number that actually matters, the one for a home that is not listed, the one nearly everyone is looking at when they check their own address out of curiosity, runs closer to 7 percent nationally. On a home worth 600,000 dollars, a market value many Carroll and Howard County homeowners are sitting on right now, a 7 percent error is a range of roughly 40,000 to 50,000 dollars. And that is the median. Half of all off-market Zestimates miss by more than that.



It also gets worse the further a property sits from a cookie-cutter subdivision with a deep pool of recent comparable sales. Rural markets, older housing stock, custom builds, and properties with acreage regularly see error rates climb into double digits. State-level data shows this variance clearly: markets with dense, uniform housing see errors as low as 5 percent, while markets with more unique, spread-out inventory see errors well above 10 percent. If that description sounds like a farm outside Taneytown or a historic property in Westminster, you are exactly the kind of homeowner this affects most.



What an Algorithm Can't See From a Server Farm



This is the part that should be obvious but somehow is not. Zillow's model pulls from public records, county tax assessments, and past sales data. It has never stood in your kitchen. It does not know if you gutted the bathrooms last spring, finished the basement, replaced the roof, or if the HVAC system is twelve years old and on borrowed time.



Kitchen and bathroom remodels, the two projects homeowners point to most often when they talk about the value they have added, frequently happen without a permit pulled. No permit means no public record. No public record means the algorithm has no idea the work ever happened. A forty thousand dollar kitchen renovation might as well not exist as far as a Zestimate is concerned.



The same blind spot shows up at the high end of the market. Automated valuation models consistently underperform on luxury and unique properties, because the things that actually drive value at that level, a premium lot, custom finishes, a specific architectural style, a setting rather than just a square footage number, are exactly the details an algorithm cannot weigh. If you own a higher end home in our market, that gap is not a minor inconvenience. It is potentially tens of thousands of dollars left on the table or a listing priced in a way that undersells what the property actually offers.



The Cautionary Tale: When Zillow Bet Real Money On Its Own Algorithm



If you want the cleanest proof that an algorithm is not a substitute for someone actually seeing a property, you do not have to take an agent's word for it. Take Zillow's.



A few years ago, Zillow decided to put the Zestimate to the ultimate test. Through a division called Zillow Offers, the company used its own valuation model to make instant cash offers on homes and buy them directly, betting that over a decade of data and refinement had made the algorithm reliable enough to price real transactions.



It had not. Zillow ended up systematically overpaying for homes it could not accurately price in a shifting market. In Phoenix alone, the large majority of homes the company had purchased were sitting on the books at a loss. By the time Zillow shut the division down entirely, the company had written off several hundred million dollars and laid off a significant share of its workforce. The company's own CEO acknowledged that the algorithm had unintentionally purchased homes at prices higher than they were actually worth.



Consider what that means. The company that literally built the Zestimate could not trust it enough to buy houses without losing money on a large share of them in some markets. That is not a criticism of Zillow's engineers. It is an honest, well documented example of what any automated valuation model can and cannot do. It can process enormous amounts of data. It cannot see a house.



Why This Matters More in Carroll, Baltimore, Howard, and Frederick Counties



Automated valuation models perform best in markets with dense, recent, highly comparable sales data, think large subdivisions built within the last fifteen years where every home shares a handful of floor plans. That is not the market most of our clients are selling in.



Carroll County alone includes working farms, equestrian properties, and homes on multiple acres where the nearest comparable sale might be a mile away and built in a completely different decade. Older neighborhoods across Baltimore and Frederick Counties carry character, custom additions, and renovation history that never made it into a public record. Howard County mixes newer high-density development with pockets of older, larger lot homes just a few streets apart, the exact kind of hyperlocal variation an algorithm averaging over a wide radius will miss.



None of this means the Zestimate is useless. It is a reasonable starting point, a rough sense of direction before you have talked to anyone. It should not be the number you use to set expectations, price a listing, or decide whether now is the right time to sell.



What an Accurate Home Valuation Actually Looks Like



A real Comparative Market Analysis is not a fifteen minute online exercise. It starts with someone walking the property, seeing the renovations that never got permitted, understanding what makes a specific street or neighborhood different from the one half a mile away, and pulling true comparable sales rather than a wide radius average that treats a 1960s farmhouse the same as a 2015 colonial.



If you are making one of the largest financial decisions of your year based on a number, that number should be built the right way. An algorithm that has, by its own creator's admission, missed badly enough to cost that creator several hundred million dollars is not where that number should come from.



Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is Zillow's Zestimate?



Zillow's own published data shows a median error rate of roughly 2 percent for homes currently listed for sale and roughly 7 percent for homes that are not listed. Because most homeowners check their Zestimate before ever listing, the more relevant number for planning purposes is the higher, off-market figure, and it can run higher still in rural markets or areas with unique properties.



Why is my Zestimate different from what my home is actually worth?



Zestimates are built from public records, tax assessments, and past sales data. They do not account for interior condition, unpermitted renovations, deferred maintenance, or the specific character of a neighborhood. Two homes with identical square footage can have very different values, and an algorithm working from a database has no way to see that difference.



What is the difference between a Zestimate and a CMA?



A Zestimate is an automated estimate generated by an algorithm with no direct knowledge of the property. A Comparative Market Analysis, or CMA, is built by a licensed agent who has walked the property, verified its condition and updates, and selected truly comparable recent sales rather than a wide statistical average. A CMA accounts for the details that actually move a sale price.



Should I trust the Zestimate when pricing my home to sell?



Use it as a rough starting point only. Pricing a home based solely on an automated estimate risks leaving money on the table if the algorithm underestimates the property, or risks an overpriced listing that sits on the market if it overestimates. An in-person evaluation from a local agent will always be more accurate than an automated model, especially in markets like Carroll, Baltimore, Howard, and Frederick Counties where property types vary widely.
