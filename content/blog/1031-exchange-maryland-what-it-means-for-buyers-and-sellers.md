Picture an investor named Paul. He is selling a Baltimore County rental he has owned for twelve years. It has gained a lot of value, and the tax bill on that gain would take a large bite out of what he can reinvest. A friend tells him about a 1031 exchange, so he lists the property and asks whether he can do one after he gets an offer.

That order is the mistake. A 1031 exchange has to be planned before closing, not after it, and the deadlines do not bend.

**Quick answer:** A 1031 exchange lets an owner defer capital gains tax on the sale of investment or business real estate by reinvesting the proceeds into replacement property. You have 45 days after closing to identify replacement property and 180 days to complete the purchase, and a qualified intermediary must hold the funds. If you are buying from someone doing an exchange, expect a seller who cares more about certainty and timing than squeezing the last dollar.

*This article is general information, not tax advice. Talk to a tax professional and a qualified intermediary before you list.*

## What Is a 1031 Exchange?

Named after Section 1031 of the tax code, a like-kind exchange lets you sell an investment property and roll the proceeds into another one without paying capital gains tax on the sale right away. The tax is deferred, not erased, and it comes due later if you eventually sell without another exchange.

## What Are the 45 Day and 180 Day Deadlines?

These two clocks start the day your sale closes and they are strict.

The identification has to be in writing, signed, and delivered to a party in the exchange, such as your qualified intermediary. Weekends, holidays, and financing delays do not extend the deadlines, except in rare cases like federally declared disasters.

You can name up to three properties in the 45 day window, regardless of value. There are other identification rules for naming more, so ask your intermediary before you plan around a longer list.

## What Should a Seller Do Before Listing?

The sellers whose exchanges go smoothly plan before the first showing.

1. **Engage a qualified intermediary before you close.** The proceeds have to go to them, not to you.
2. **Start looking for replacement property while the sale is under contract,** not after closing. Forty-five days go quickly.
3. **Build your team early:** the intermediary, a tax professional or attorney, and an agent who has worked with exchange timelines.
4. **Tell your agent and put it in the contract.** Buyers should be notified of your intent to do an exchange and that you will need their cooperation with the closing process.

## What If the Replacement Property Needs to Be Built?

This is where many exchanges get into trouble. If you plan to buy land and build a property, the 180 day clock still applies, while permitting and construction in Maryland often take 9 to 12 months.

An exchange like this is possible, but it is more complex. It is typically handled through a "build to suit" structure in which an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder holds title while the work happens. Two points matter most:

- Only what is built and in place by day 180 counts toward the value of the replacement property, and it needs to match or exceed the value of what you sold to defer all of the tax.
- Permitting has to be planned from the start, because the clock does not care how long the county takes.

Do not attempt this without a qualified intermediary and a tax advisor who have done it before.

## What If You Are Buying From a 1031 Exchanger?

A seller in an exchange is on a deadline they cannot move, so certainty often matters more to them than the last dollar. If you are the buyer, that changes how to make your offer strong.

- **A strong pre-approval and few contingencies** make your offer more attractive.
- **Expect a firm closing date.** The IRS timeline usually leaves little room to shift it.
- **Your standard protections still apply.** Inspection and appraisal contingencies remain your rights, but expect less patience with drawn-out negotiations.
- **Cooperate with the paperwork.** The seller's intermediary will need some documents and language from you at closing.

## The Bottom Line

A 1031 exchange can save a serious amount of tax, but only for those who plan. The people who get into trouble are not people who do not understand the idea. They are people who start thinking about it after they have accepted an offer. If you own investment property and you are thinking about selling, call before you list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a 1031 exchange in simple terms?**

It is a way to sell investment real estate and defer the capital gains tax by reinvesting the proceeds into another qualifying property. The tax is postponed, not eliminated.

**How long do I have to complete a 1031 exchange?**

You have 45 days from closing to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days from closing to complete the purchase. These deadlines are generally not extendable.

**Can I do a 1031 exchange on my primary residence?**

No. The property must be held for investment or business use. A home used only as your primary residence does not qualify, though some vacation properties that are rented can qualify under IRS guidelines.

**Do I need a qualified intermediary?**

For a standard delayed exchange, yes. The intermediary holds the sale proceeds so you never take control of the money.

**How many properties can I identify?**

You can generally name up to three properties within the 45 days, regardless of their value. There is also a rule allowing more, based on total value, so confirm the details with your intermediary.
